Basketball Lower Back Pain.

You play two hours on Saturday. Sunday morning you sit on the edge of the bed for a minute before you trust your back to stand up. A lot of adult hoopers know that morning. This page is about what usually sits underneath it, and what to train.

Before You Read
"Already seeing a pro for your back? Keep doing that. This is training, not treatment."

First: When This Isn't a Training Question

Nothing on this page diagnoses anything, and nothing here replaces somebody putting hands on you. Some back pain is a reason to stop reading and get checked. Book that appointment first if any of these are true:

  • The pain started with a fall, a hard hit, or a single moment you felt something go
  • Pain, numbness or tingling runs down one or both legs
  • A leg feels weak, or your foot catches on the floor
  • It's getting worse week to week instead of settling
  • It wakes you up at night, or comes with fever or changes in your bladder or bowels

If none of that fits, and what you have is the familiar ache after runs that settles in a day or two, keep reading. That pattern is common, and for most hoopers it responds to training.

Why Hoopers Feel It in the Back

Basketball asks a lot of the hips. You sit into a defensive stance, you hinge down for loose balls, you rotate to make a pass, you land and go again. When the hips have the range and the strength to do that work, the lower back mostly rides along.

The problem is what most adult hoopers do with the other 160 hours of the week. You sit at work. You sit in the car. You sit on the couch. The front of the hip gets used to being short, and the glutes get used to doing very little. Then Saturday asks those same hips for deep, fast, loaded range.

When the hips can't give it, the body finds the motion somewhere else. For a lot of hoopers, that somewhere is the lower back. It bends where the hips should hinge. It twists where the hips should turn. Ninety minutes of that and the back lets you know about it, usually the next morning.

The Truth
"For a lot of hoopers, the back is doing the hips' job. Train the hips and the back gets its own job back."

That's not the answer for every back. It's the most common pattern BTH sees in adult hoopers, and it's why the work below starts at the hips.

What Tends to Make It Worse

Toe-touch stretching before you play. Folding over and pulling on a sore back feels productive. It mostly asks the same area that's already doing too much to do more. If you want to move before a run, move the hips and ankles.

Full rest, then a full run. A week off quiets the ache. Then you come back to two hours of pickup with the same hips you left with, and the ache comes back on schedule. Rest by itself doesn't change what the hips can do.

Sitting right after you play. Two hours of pickup, then forty minutes folded into a car seat. Stand up, walk for five minutes, and let things cool down before you sit.

Only doing crunches. Most hoopers think "core" means sit-ups. On the court the job of your trunk is mostly to hold still while your hips and arms move. Crunches train the opposite.

What to Train Instead

1. Hip range you can control. Hip CARs daily. A loaded couch stretch with the glute squeezed. Slow goblet squats with a pause at the bottom. The goal is range the hip owns, so the back doesn't have to supply it. The full breakdown is in hip mobility for hoopers.

2. Glutes that actually work. Hip thrusts, glute bridges with a hold at the top, single-leg bridges. Two or three sets, two or three times a week. Most hoopers are surprised how hard a slow single-leg bridge is.

3. A trunk that resists motion. Dead bugs. Side planks. Suitcase carries, where you walk holding one heavy weight on one side and don't let it pull you over. These teach the trunk to stay put while the hips work, which is the job it has on the court.

4. A hinge you can trust. Light Romanian deadlifts with a dowel or a light kettlebell. Push the hips back, keep the back long, feel it in the hamstrings. This is the pattern you use every time you drop for a loose ball. Start light and build over time.

5. Walking. Not glamorous. For a lot of adults with a cranky back, an easy 20 to 30 minute walk on off days does more than anything on a foam roller.

If any of these hurts sharply, back off the range or the load until it doesn't. Mild effort is fine. Sharp pain is information.

A Simple Week Around Pickup

Say you play Tuesday and Saturday. A week that works for most hoopers looks something like this:

  • Monday: 20 minutes. Hip CARs, glute bridges, dead bugs, side planks.
  • Tuesday: 8-minute warmup, then play. Walk five minutes before you sit.
  • Wednesday: Easy walk, 20 to 30 minutes.
  • Thursday: 25 minutes. Light RDLs, goblet squats with pause, suitcase carries, single-leg bridges.
  • Friday: Rest or an easy walk.
  • Saturday: Warmup, play, walk before you sit.
  • Sunday: Easy walk and 5 minutes of hip CARs.

That's under two hours of work outside of pickup. The warmup is in the best basketball warmup guide. Give it a few weeks before you judge it, and keep the red-flag list at the top of this page in mind the whole way.

Quick Answers

Why does my lower back hurt after basketball?

For a lot of adult hoopers, it's the back doing work the hips can't. Hips that sit all week can't give the range the game asks for, so the lower back bends and twists to cover it. If the pain is sharp, runs down a leg, or keeps getting worse, get it checked first.

Should I stop playing basketball if my lower back hurts?

If it started with a fall or a hard hit, runs down a leg, or keeps getting worse, stop and get it checked. For the day-after ache that settles in a day or two, most hoopers do better training the hips and trunk than sitting out and coming back to the same hips.

Is stretching good for lower back pain from basketball?

Gentle movement usually feels better than sitting still. Pulling hard on a sore back with toe touches mostly asks it to do more. Move the hips instead, and build the trunk with dead bugs, side planks and carries.
